Concept 🧠:
Each wine has 9 chemistry numbers. You can draw 2 numbers on a flat graph (one across, one up), but you can't draw 9. 
tSNE solves this: it makes a flat map where each wine is one dot, and wines with similar chemistry are placed close together.
Think of a wedding seating plan: guests with similar interests are seated near each other, so friend groups end up at the same tables. 
tSNE does that with wines.
Then you colour each dot by its quality score. 
If good wines bunch together on the map, that tells you good wines have similar chemistry.


Three things you need to know:
1️⃣ Leave quality out of the map. tSNE should place wines using only the 9 chemistry numbers. 
Quality is used only for the colour. If quality were included, good wines would cluster just because they share a score, 
and the picture would tell us nothing new.
2️⃣ Scale the numbers first ("standardise"). The columns use very different units. 
Total sulfur dioxide goes up to about 289, while density barely moves around 0.99. 
Without scaling, tSNE would mostly look at the big numbers and ignore the small ones. 
It's like comparing people by height in centimetres and weight in tonnes: height would dominate just because its numbers are bigger. 
Standardising puts every column on the same scale.
3️⃣ Perplexity is a tSNE setting that roughly means how many neighbours each wine pays attention to when placing itself. 
The default is 30, and the task's tip suggests 60 may separate this data better.

Perplexity comparison:

Perplexity 30:
- The high-quality wines (blue ▲) split into several small clumps across the top: top-left, top-middle and top-right.
- The low-quality wines (red ▼) gather on the left edge, but many are scattered through the middle and bottom.

Perplexity 60:
- The high-quality wines form one main region on the right, and the low-quality wines sit mostly on the left.
- The split between low and high is easier to see at a glance.

What it means: perplexity is roughly how many neighbours each wine "pays attention to". 
At 30, tSNE focuses on very close neighbours, so it shows many small local groups. 
At 60, each wine considers more neighbours, so the bigger overall pattern (good on one side, poor on the other) shows more clearly. 
Both pictures agree on the main finding: good and poor wines tend to sit in different areas, 
but they still overlap with the grey medium wines. So chemistry is related to quality, but doesn't fully decide it.
